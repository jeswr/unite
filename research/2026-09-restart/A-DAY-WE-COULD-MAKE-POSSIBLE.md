# A day we could make possible

An optimistic design fiction for Unite · 27 September 2026

**What if a good life were something we helped one another make possible, and everyone could help shape how?**

The people, events and outcomes below are invented. This is one possible future to discuss, amend or reject—not a prediction, a medical promise, a chosen political programme or a demonstration of what the prototype already delivers. Its [research and design rationale](LIFE-FIRST-UNITE.md) distinguishes evidence from aspiration.

## An ordinary day

Maya wakes naturally. She likes the quiet before the rest of her home stirs. Breakfast is something she makes slowly with her daughter, who wants to talk about a creature she invented yesterday. There is no shift to rush to, no application to prove deservingness, and no worry that taking time to listen means losing their home.

Several years earlier, Maya had described what she wanted as “enough income to stop falling behind.” Unite had asked what that would allow her to do. She spoke about breakfast, living near people she loved, making things with her hands and being able to go outside on days when movement was difficult. Money was a real barrier then. It had never been the whole aspiration.

Today she chooses a morning at a shared workshop. A vehicle with the access features she selected is available, but she decides to stay nearby. Her health still varies. The future did not demand that she become a different kind of person before it could make room for her. She can change the day without asking permission or losing support.

The workshop has tools she could not once have obtained. Automated systems handle much of the preparation and heavy lifting. Maya enjoys learning to shape wood, so that part remains hers. Someone else prefers a completely automated result. Both choices are welcome. Making something is a pleasure and a relationship, not an entry requirement for food or shelter.

At lunch she joins Noor and a few neighbours in a garden. Noor likes teaching and meeting people, but dislikes being obliged to repeat exhausting administrative tasks to keep doing it. Assistance with planning and routine preparation has freed up time. Some people prepare the meal because they enjoy cooking; others have food prepared automatically. People who need particular food can choose it without having to explain themselves to the group.

Their community does not use a price or an earnings test to decide whether someone may eat, live securely, learn or receive essential support. In this imagined setting, the necessary productive capacity and shared access arrangements have been built over time. Neither AI capability alone nor an instruction to “share fairly” was enough to accomplish that.

In the afternoon Maya helps redesign a shaded path. She likes this work but could decline it. A neighbour, Eli, has asked for quiet beside his home; another wants space for music and gathering. Instead of requiring one to lose, the group examines a sheltered gathering space further along the path and a quiet route closer to Eli. People who use mobility aids and people who maintain the area help evaluate it. A technical team checks the ground and water requirements. The first plan changes after they find a problem.

Nobody asks an AI to decide whose life matters more. The tools make alternatives and consequences easier to understand. The affected people agree on a reversible trial under rules they can inspect and contest. If agreement fails, the unresolved question remains visible and goes through their chosen dispute process. The system does not manufacture a unanimous answer.

Maya spends a few minutes reviewing the proposal in her preferred way. Eli prefers an in-person conversation; an advocate conveys another person's concerns with permission. Someone else delegates this week's routine decisions to a person they trust. They can withdraw that delegation. Receiving support is never conditional on attending meetings or endorsing the plan.

At home that evening, Maya plays with her daughter and calls a friend. Her personal assistant offers to ask whether the day worked for her. She says “not now.” It stops. Tomorrow she may want something different.

## What made this possible?

The visible experience is simple; the supporting arrangements took collective effort.

**People defined what mattered.** Maya's account stayed hers to change. Health information remained private unless she chose to disclose it for a specific purpose. She could share “I need a step-free route” without sharing a diagnosis. Tools connected related public requests without turning private lives into public profiles.

**People compared more than one route.** Early improvements included money and existing services because those were real ways to help at the time. Other experiments removed the need to buy particular things individually. Over time, some communities chose forms of shared provision that made earning income unnecessary for essentials. Other communities kept different arrangements while meeting the same guarantees.

**Automation's gains became usable opportunities.** Tools, energy, materials, maintenance and human expertise were made available under agreements people could influence. People measured who gained time and access and who still did not. A system that produced more while concentrating control would have failed the purpose even if its output was impressive.

**Coordination became a shared, revisable practice.** Neighbourhoods, resource networks and communities of knowledge could make and change limited agreements. Cross-boundary effects—such as shared water—required participation beyond the immediate group. There was no single permanent authority choosing everyone's life. Some people still held clearly limited responsibilities for reliability, expertise, emergency response and resolving disputes, subject to review and replacement.

**Unite could be replaced.** Several independent tools could communicate. Communities could move their records, choose another model, change their interface or work offline. The people maintaining software could not quietly become the world's governors. A person could leave the platform and still receive essential support.

This scenario imagines functions now bundled into governments and firms being reorganised. It leaves open which names, structures or boundaries people might retain. It does not confuse removal of today's institutions with removal of the need to coordinate, protect rights and take responsibility.

## A sample outcome Unite could help people create

An earlier conversation might have gone like this. This dialogue is authored fiction, not output from the AI or a real interview:

**Maya:** “I'd need more money before I could think about any of this.”

**Unite:** “What is one thing that money would make possible in an ordinary day?”

**Maya:** “Taking my daughter to make things together. I used to love that.”

**Unite:** “What do you most miss about those times?”

**Maya:** “Being absorbed in something together, without hurrying.”

**Unite:** “What gets in the way of having that kind of time now?”

**Maya:** “The tools cost a lot, but actually the bigger problem is getting there. Some days I need help, and everything has to be booked ahead.”

**Unite:** “It sounds as though being able to decide on the day matters as much as having the tools—is that right?”

**Maya:** “Yes. And I don't always want someone helping. Sometimes I want to figure it out myself.”

**Unite:** “If those arrangements could be different, what would you want to choose for yourself?”

The conversation has not decided that money is irrelevant, that Maya needs a particular service or that she wants any political model. It has made a more useful question visible: how could shared creative time become accessible on terms she chooses?

This is a **fictional co-design record** showing the path from an experience to an experiment. It contains no actual participant findings or commitments.

| Part of the record | Example |
|---|---|
| Person's words | “I want to share breakfast without rushing and get to the workshop even on a difficult day.” |
| Confirmed meaning | Time with family, chosen creative activity, dependable access and freedom to change plans |
| What must remain | Choice over assistance; privacy; no obligation to produce anything |
| Current barriers | Unpredictable time demands, unsuitable route, individually costly tools, support that must be booked too far ahead |
| Unknowns | Which barrier matters most to Maya; whether others want the same solution; actual access and resource requirements |
| Several possibilities | Change schedules; share nearby tools; provide adaptable transport; bring activities closer; redesign how support is allocated |
| Affected people | Participants, carers, neighbours, workshop maintainers, transport providers, people currently unable to join |
| A present experiment | Co-design two flexible workshop mornings with existing local resources, while retaining people's current support |
| Real commitments needed | A willing host, accessible space and route, materials, reliable assistance, agreed responsibilities; present-day costs accounted for honestly |
| Recognition of success | Maya can choose whether to attend and reports the day fits what she wanted; other participants and helpers report their effects, including burdens |
| Reasons to revise or stop | Support proves unreliable, neighbours are harmed, helpers are overburdened, or Maya changes her mind |
| Longer question | Could dependable access become ordinary, with less dependence on individual purchasing power? |

The first action is deliberately smaller than the vision. It tests one enabling condition while preserving the larger question. A successful morning would not establish that the whole imagined economy works.

## Questions that keep the vision open

What would your own day look like if it did not have to fit the present system? What would you preserve from life now? What would you still want to do yourself? What would you like help with, and from whom? Whose choices might your vision constrain? What would make it possible for different good lives to coexist?

Even with abundant production, not every desire can be jointly satisfied. A particular place, another person's attention, ecological capacity or an unproven treatment may remain unavailable. Unite's promise should be to help people understand and improve their possibilities together, with honest limits and revisable arrangements—not to certify a perfect life or total agreement.

The optimistic core is that **far more of what makes life worthwhile could become reliably available to everyone, while everyone gains a meaningful say in how that happens**.
